# 02 — Roots, Deepest Layer: Human Evolution ("The Taproot")

**Where it lives on the tree:** The **deepest root** (the taproot) at the very bottom of the underground world. Time here runs from **~3.4 million years ago** to the end of the Ice Age. The deeper the visitor digs, the older the layer (like real archaeology, where deeper soil = older). The HUD year counter shows things like `3,390,000 YEARS AGO`.

**Assignment requirement covered:** Background: "what caused the issue." This is the oldest root of the whole argument: *what did humans evolve to eat?* Both sides of today's debate point back here, so present it as a **contested** layer, not a settled one.

## Layer intro (shows when the visitor reaches this layer)

> **THE TAPROOT: What were humans built to eat?**
> Everyone in this debate eventually says "it's natural." Meat-eaters say humans evolved as hunters. Vegetarians say our ancestors mostly ate plants. The fossil record is messier, and more interesting, than either side admits.

---

### LEAF evo-1 — The First Butcher Marks (~3.4 million years ago)
- **Type:** history / evidence
- **Text:** In 2010, scientists found two animal bones at Dikika, Ethiopia, scratched with what look like stone-tool cut marks, nearly **3.4 million years old**. The only human relative living there was *Australopithecus afarensis*, the species of the famous "Lucy" fossil. That would push meat-eating back about 800,000 years, though no stone tools were found at the site and scientists still argue about it [src:wong-dikika] [src:mcpherron2010].
- **Pixel scene:** A tiny hunched hominin sprite crouches over a bone, tapping it with a rock. Dust particles puff on each tap. A "?" bubble floats over the bone (to show the evidence is debated).
- **Sources:** [src:wong-dikika] [src:mcpherron2010]

### LEAF evo-2 — "Meat Made Us Human" (the classic theory)
- **Type:** claim (supports Position 3)
- **Text:** For decades, a popular theory said **meat fed our big brains**. The "Expensive-Tissue Hypothesis" (1995) argued that brains use a lot of energy, so early humans needed rich food like meat. That let their guts shrink while their brains grew [src:aiello1995]. This idea is a favorite of meat-eaters today: we're meat-eaters by design.
- **Pixel scene:** A cartoon pixel brain inflates like a balloon while a pixel steak shrinks beside it. A gut-icon shrinks too.
- **Sources:** [src:aiello1995]

### LEAF evo-3 — Not So Fast: the 2022 Recount
- **Type:** evidence (complicates Position 3)
- **Text:** In 2022, researchers re-counted butchered bones from sites in East Africa dated **2.6 to 1.2 million years ago**. They found that there are more butchered bones in later sites mostly because scientists *dug more* at those sites. Once they corrected for that, there was **no lasting increase** in meat-eating when *Homo erectus* appeared. Lead author Andrew Barr: "Our study undermines the idea that eating large quantities of meat drove evolutionary changes in our early ancestors" [src:barr2022].
- **Pixel scene:** A pixel archaeologist with a clipboard and a tally counter: "More digging = more bones found!"
- **Sources:** [src:barr2022]

### LEAF evo-4 — Fire Changed Everything
- **Type:** history / evidence
- **Text:** Harvard anthropologist Richard Wrangham argues that the real game-changer was **cooking**. Fire makes both meat *and* starchy plants softer and easier to digest, which unlocks more energy for a growing brain [src:wrangham2009] [src:harvard-wrangham2009]. The 2022 researchers also suggested cooking, and plant foods gathered by grandmothers, as possible drivers of human evolution [src:barr2022].
- **Pixel scene:** An animated pixel campfire (flicker loop) with hominin sprites sitting around it roasting both a bone and a root tuber on sticks. Firelight glow pulses on the cave wall.
- **Sources:** [src:wrangham2009] [src:harvard-wrangham2009] [src:barr2022]

### LEAF evo-5 — Even Neanderthals Ate Their Veggies
- **Type:** evidence
- **Text:** Neanderthals are often pictured as pure meat-eaters. But scientists who studied the hardened plaque on Neanderthal teeth from Shanidar Cave (Iraq) and Spy (Belgium) found tiny bits of **cooked plants and grains** stuck in it [src:henry2011].
- **Pixel scene:** A giant pixel tooth with a magnifying glass revealing tiny green and tan grains.
- **Sources:** [src:henry2011]

### LEAF evo-6 — The Smithsonian's Verdict: Flexible Omnivores
- **Type:** claim (common ground)
- **Text:** The Smithsonian's Human Origins Program describes early humans as **omnivores whose diets changed with their environment**. They ate whatever was available, from tubers and fruit to scavenged or hunted meat [src:smithsonian-tools-food]. This is the closest thing to common ground in the whole debate: humans *can* eat meat, and humans *can* thrive on plants. "Natural" covers a lot.
- **Pixel scene:** A split diorama: one half savanna with fruit trees, the other half tundra with reindeer. The same hominin sprite walks between them and changes what it carries.
- **Sources:** [src:smithsonian-tools-food]

### LEAF evo-7 — Kiviaq: Meat That Waits
- **Type:** history (culture)
- **Text:** In the Arctic, where few plants grow, people depended on animals. The Inughuit of northwest Greenland still make **kiviaq**: hundreds of little auks (small seabirds) packed into a hollowed-out **seal skin**, sealed with fat, and buried under rocks to ferment for **three to six months** or more [src:atlas-kiviaq]. The little auk has been crucial to life in that region for a long time [src:mosbech2018]. It's a reminder that "the human diet" depends on where humans live.
- **Pixel scene:** Snowy pixel landscape; a figure in fur clothing stacks stones over a seal-shaped mound; a calendar icon flips "1 month… 2 months… 3 months…"
- **Sources:** [src:atlas-kiviaq] [src:mosbech2018]
- **Accuracy note:** The original paper said whales were buried 6 feet underground. The sources describe **seals** placed **under rocks**. Use the corrected version.

### LEAF evo-8 — When Hunting Outran Nature
- **Type:** history (supports Position 2's "we can overdo it")
- **Text:** Humans can also eat animals faster than nature can replace them. Before colonization, an estimated **30 to 60 million bison** roamed the Great Plains. Westward expansion, the market for bison, and a deliberate U.S. government effort to wipe them out (to weaken the Native nations that depended on them) left just **512 plains bison by 1889** [src:wwf-bison] [src:nps-bison].
- **Pixel scene:** A herd of tiny pixel bison stampeding across the screen, thinning out as a year counter ticks forward. *(This is a modern event; you can also place it in the trunk timeline, see `05-trunk-modern-history.md`.)*
- **Sources:** [src:wwf-bison] [src:nps-bison]
- **Accuracy note:** The original paper said the population fell to "nearly 100,000." The low point was in the **hundreds**, not 100,000. Use the corrected number.

## Layer takeaway (end-of-layer banner)

> **ROOT LESSON:** Evolution is the oldest argument in the debate, and both sides misuse it. Humans clearly ate meat for millions of years, but the "meat made us human" story is weaker than it sounds, and plants and cooking mattered too. "Natural" doesn't settle what we *should* eat today.
